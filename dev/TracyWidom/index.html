Tracy–Widom Distributions

The Tracy–Widom law $F_\beta$ is the limiting distribution of the largest eigenvalue of a Gaussian $\beta$-ensemble, and — after the twScaling centering — of the largest eigenvalue of a Wishart matrix. The distribution is computed from the Hastings–McLeod solution of Painlevé II; published means are $-1.20653$ ($\beta = 1$), $-1.77109$ ($\beta = 2$) and $-2.30688$ ($\beta = 4$).

RandomMatrix.TracyWidom — Type
TracyWidom(beta)

The Tracy-Widom distribution F_beta: the limiting law of the largest eigenvalue of a Gaussian beta-ensemble, and (with the twScaling centering) of the largest eigenvalue of a Wishart matrix. beta is 1 (real), 2 (complex) or 4 (symplectic) for cdf/pdf/quantile; those are computed from the Hastings-McLeod Painleve II function. For general beta > 0, rand samples the Dumitriu-Edelman tridiagonal model (Monte-Carlo accuracy) and cdf/pdf/quantile are unavailable.

Mean and variance (beta = 1, 2, 4): -1.20653/1.60778, -1.77109/0.81319, -2.30688/0.51772.

RandomMatrix.twScaling — Function
twScaling(n, p)

Johnstone's centering and scaling (mu, sigma) for the largest eigenvalue of the normalized sample covariance (1/n) X'X of an n x p standard Gaussian data matrix: (lambda_max - mu) / sigma converges to TracyWidom(1) (real data; TracyWidom(2) for complex data), with

mu    = (sqrt(n-1) + sqrt(p))^2 / n
sigma = (sqrt(n-1) + sqrt(p)) (1/sqrt(n-1) + 1/sqrt(p))^(1/3) / n
source
RandomMatrix.twTest — Function
twTest(lambda, n, p; beta=1)

Johnstone's Tracy-Widom test of the largest sample eigenvalue: is the largest eigenvalue lambda of the normalized sample covariance (1/n) X'X of an n x p Gaussian data matrix consistent with the null of identity population covariance (pure noise), or is it a signal outlier?

Returns a NamedTuple (statistic, pvalue) with the scaled statistic

statistic = (lambda - mu) / sigma,     (mu, sigma) = twScaling(n, p)

and the upper-tail p-value pvalue = 1 - F_beta(statistic) under TracyWidom: a small p-value is evidence that lambda sits above the noise bulk. beta = 1 (default) is for real data, beta = 2 for complex data; Johnstone's scaling covers these two cases.

The null has noise variance 1; for data with noise variance sigma2, apply the test to lambda / sigma2.

Reference: Johnstone, "On the distribution of the largest eigenvalue in principal components analysis", Ann. Statist. 29(2), 2001, DOI 10.1214/aos/1009210544.

RandomMatrix.BetaHermite — Type
BetaHermite(beta, n)

The Gaussian beta-ensemble: the eigenvalue distribution of an n x n Dumitriu–Edelman tridiagonal matrix, with joint density

prod_{i<j} |λᵢ - λⱼ|^beta * exp(-beta/4 * sumᵢ λᵢ^2).

rand returns the n eigenvalues as a sorted vector; it is a Distributions multivariate distribution, so rand(rng, d, k) draws k spectra as the columns of an n x k matrix. beta = 1, 2, 4 are the classical orthogonal / unitary / symplectic cases (the eigenvalue law of a GOE / GUE / GSE matrix in this scaling), and any beta > 0 is allowed.

The empirical distribution of λ / sqrt(n) converges to the semicircle law on [-2, 2], and the rescaled largest eigenvalue n^(1/6) (λmax - 2 sqrt(n)) converges to TracyWidom(beta) (Ramírez–Rider–Virág) — with one exception: at beta = 4 that rescaled variable converges to the stochastic-Airy normalization of TW_4 (mean -2.05520, var 0.41092), and it converges to the classic TracyWidom(4) (mean -2.30688, var 0.51772) only after multiplication by 2^(1/6). The exact identity E[sum λᵢ^2] = 2n/beta + n(n-1) holds at every n, and mean(d) = zeros(n) by the symmetry λ -> -λ of the density.

rand(BetaHermite(2, 100))        # one spectrum of 100 eigenvalues
rand(BetaHermite(0.5, 50), 200)  # 200 spectra as a 50 x 200 matrix

Reference: Dumitriu & Edelman, "Matrix models for beta ensembles", J. Math. Phys. 43(12), 2002, DOI 10.1063/1.1507823.

RandomMatrix.BetaLaguerre — Type
BetaLaguerre(beta, n, p)

The Wishart beta-ensemble: the eigenvalue distribution of an unnormalized p x p Gram matrix with n observations, sampled from the Dumitriu–Edelman bidiagonal model. The joint density is

prodᵢ λᵢ^(beta(n-p+1)/2 - 1) * exp(-beta/2 * sumᵢ λᵢ)
    * prod_{i<j} |λᵢ - λⱼ|^beta,     λᵢ >= 0,

so at beta = 1 and beta = 2 these are exactly the eigenvalues of a real and a complex Wishart matrix X'X with X an n x p standard Gaussian matrix. rand returns the p eigenvalues as a sorted vector; rand(rng, d, k) draws k spectra as the columns of a p x k matrix. Requires n >= 1 and p >= 1. For n < p the Gram matrix is singular of rank n, and rand returns its spectrum in the same form: p - n exact zeros plus n positive eigenvalues which, by the Gram swap between X'X and XX', follow the joint density above with n and p exchanged.

The exact identity E[sum λᵢ] = n p holds at every beta, so the eigenvalues divided by n have mean 1 and their empirical distribution converges to MarchenkoPastur(p/n); the largest eigenvalue, centered with twScaling(n, p), follows TracyWidom(beta) in the same sense as for BetaHermite (including its beta = 4 2^(1/6) caveat).

rand(BetaLaguerre(1, 400, 100)) ./ 400   # one Wishart spectrum, MP scale

Reference: Dumitriu & Edelman, "Matrix models for beta ensembles", J. Math. Phys. 43(12), 2002, DOI 10.1063/1.1507823.

Worked example

TracyWidom is a Distributions distribution, so mean, var, cdf and quantile work as usual; the mean of $F_2$ is the published $-1.77109$ quoted above, so its cdf there is close to $1/2$.

using RandomMatrix, Distributions
d = TracyWidom(2)
(mean = round(mean(d), digits = 5), var = round(var(d), digits = 5),
 cdf_at_mean = round(cdf(d, mean(d)), digits = 3),
 q95 = round(quantile(d, 0.95), digits = 3))
(mean = -1.77109, var = 0.81319, cdf_at_mean = 0.515, q95 = -0.232)

For a Wishart matrix, twScaling gives the Johnstone centering and scaling to apply to the largest eigenvalue before comparing with $F_1$ (real data):

(mu, sigma) = twScaling(400, 100)
(mu = round(mu, digits = 3), sigma = round(sigma, digits = 4))
(mu = 2.246, sigma = 0.0398)

Testing the largest eigenvalue

twTest wires the two ingredients together into Johnstone's test of the largest sample eigenvalue against the pure-noise null (identity population covariance): it returns the scaled statistic $(\lambda - \mu)/\sigma$ and its upper-tail p-value under $F_1$. At $n = 400$, $p = 100$ an observed $\lambda = 2.28$ is borderline (p-value $0.06$), while $\lambda = 2.35$ is a clear signal outlier:

r = twTest(2.28, 400, 100)
(statistic = round(r.statistic, digits = 3), pvalue = round(r.pvalue, digits = 3))
(statistic = 0.848, pvalue = 0.06)
r = twTest(2.35, 400, 100)
(statistic = round(r.statistic, digits = 3), pvalue = round(r.pvalue, digits = 4))
(statistic = 2.605, pvalue = 0.0035)

Testing the largest MANOVA root

For a double Wishart (MANOVA) problem, Roy's greatest-root test asks the same question about the largest eigenvalue $\theta$ of $(A + B)^{-1} B$, with $A \sim W_p(I, m)$ the error Wishart and $B \sim W_p(I, n)$ the hypothesis Wishart. Johnstone (2008) showed that the logit transform $W = \log(\theta / (1 - \theta))$ is Tracy–Widom after centering and scaling — the angles in the constants carry $-1/2$ / $-1$ corrections, which make the approximation second-order accurate:

RandomMatrix.manovaTWScaling — Function
manovaTWScaling(p, m, n; beta=1)

Johnstone's centering and scaling (mu, sigma) for the greatest root test of manovaTWTest: with theta the largest eigenvalue of (A + B)⁻¹ B for independent A ~ W_p(I, m) (error) and B ~ W_p(I, n) (hypothesis) Wisharts, the logit transform W = log(theta / (1 - theta)) satisfies

(W - mu) / sigma  ->  TracyWidom(beta)

in distribution as p, m, n grow in proportion. For real data (beta = 1), mu = 2 log tan((phi + gamma) / 2) and sigma^3 = 16 (m + n - 1)^{-2} / (sin^2(phi + gamma) sin(phi) sin(gamma)) with angles sin^2(gamma/2) = (min(p, n) - 1/2) / (m + n - 1) and sin^2(phi/2) = (max(p, n) - 1/2) / (m + n - 1) — eqs. (5)–(6) of Johnstone (2008), whose -1/2 and -1 corrections give the approximation its second-order, O(p^{-2/3}), accuracy. For complex data (beta = 2) the constants are the weighted averages of Theorem 2 of the same paper.

Reference: Johnstone, "Multivariate analysis and Jacobi ensembles: largest eigenvalue, Tracy–Widom limits and rates of convergence", Ann. Statist. 36(6), 2008, DOI 10.1214/08-AOS605.

RandomMatrix.manovaTWTest — Function
manovaTWTest(theta, p, m, n; beta=1)

Johnstone's Tracy–Widom greatest-root test for a MANOVA (double Wishart) problem: is the largest eigenvalue theta of (A + B)⁻¹ B — with A ~ W_p(I, m) the error Wishart on m degrees of freedom and B ~ W_p(I, n) the hypothesis Wishart on n degrees of freedom, independent with a common covariance under the null — consistent with pure noise, or evidence of signal (Roy's largest-root test with a Tracy–Widom p-value)?

Returns a NamedTuple (statistic, pvalue) with

statistic = (log(theta / (1 - theta)) - mu) / sigma,
(mu, sigma) = manovaTWScaling(p, m, n; beta)

and the upper-tail p-value pvalue = 1 - F_beta(statistic) under TracyWidom: a small p-value is evidence that the top MANOVA root sits above the noise bulk. beta = 1 (default) is for real data, beta = 2 for complex data.

Mind the package's other MANOVA convention: the Wachter law and the spiked-MANOVA functions describe (A + B)⁻¹ A with A ~ W_p(Σ, n₁) the numerator Wishart. The largest eigenvalue x of that matrix is the greatest root with error degrees of freedom n₂ and hypothesis degrees of freedom n₁, so test it as manovaTWTest(x, p, n₂, n₁).

The approximation is second-order (O(p^{-2/3})) in the regime of Johnstone's condition (3): p, m, n grow in proportion with min(p, n) / (m + n) bounded away from 0 and p < m. It was calibrated against simulation before the test bands were set: at (p, m, n) = (20, 160, 40), (50, 100, 50), (40, 120, 20) and (30, 60, 90) the scaled statistic's mean and sd match the Tracy–Widom values within Monte Carlo error and the 5% rejection rate falls in 0.040–0.054 (real) and 0.043–0.047 (complex).

This is the balanced regime: for the unbalanced complex regime see jacobiTWScaling.

Reference: Johnstone, "Multivariate analysis and Jacobi ensembles: largest eigenvalue, Tracy–Widom limits and rates of convergence", Ann. Statist. 36(6), 2008, DOI 10.1214/08-AOS605.

source

At $p = 50$, $m = 100$, $n = 50$ the logit-scale centering is $\mu \approx 2.064$ with scale $\sigma \approx 0.127$; an observed root $\theta = 0.88$ is consistent with noise (p-value $0.294$), while $\theta = 0.90$ is a borderline signal (p-value $0.045$):

(mu, sigma) = manovaTWScaling(50, 100, 50)
(mu = round(mu, digits = 4), sigma = round(sigma, digits = 4))
(mu = 2.0644, sigma = 0.1267)
r = manovaTWTest(0.88, 50, 100, 50)
(statistic = round(r.statistic, digits = 3), pvalue = round(r.pvalue, digits = 3))
(statistic = -0.568, pvalue = 0.294)
r = manovaTWTest(0.90, 50, 100, 50)
(statistic = round(r.statistic, digits = 3), pvalue = round(r.pvalue, digits = 3))
(statistic = 1.049, pvalue = 0.045)

One convention warning: the package's Wachter law and spiked MANOVA functions describe $(A + B)^{-1} A$ with $A$ the numerator Wishart on $n_1$ degrees of freedom. The largest eigenvalue $x$ of that matrix is the greatest root with error degrees of freedom $n_2$ and hypothesis degrees of freedom $n_1$ — test it as manovaTWTest(x, p, n₂, n₁).

The unbalanced Jacobi edge

For the Jacobi (MANOVA) matrix $J = (A + B)^{-1} B$ of two independent complex Wisharts, the largest eigenvalue still has Tracy–Widom(2) fluctuations at the right edge — but in the unbalanced regime $n - p = b\, p$, $m - p = a\, p^{1+\nu}$ ($\nu > 0$), where one Wishart is much fatter than the other, the centering is not Johnstone's. Didier, Gkogkou, Lopez Santander & McLaughlin (arXiv:2611.00577, 2026) give the finite-$p$ location and scale explicitly, together with an explicit $p^{-2/3}$ correction to the distribution function (their Theorem 3.1):

RandomMatrix.jacobiTWScaling — Function
jacobiTWScaling(p, a, b, nu) -> (location, scale)

Location and scale for the largest eigenvalue θmax of the unbalanced complex Jacobi (MANOVA) matrix J = (A + B)⁻¹ B, with A ~ CW_p(I, m), B ~ CW_p(I, n) independent complex Wisharts, in the regime (eq. (1.8) of Didier et al. 2026)

\[n - p = b\, p, \qquad m - p = a\, p^{1 + \nu}, \qquad a, b > 0, \ \nu > 0,\]

so one Wishart is much fatter than the other. The location is the right endpoint β_p of the equilibrium support (eq. (2.12)) and the scale is σ_p = Č_p p^{-2/3} with the finite-p coefficient Č_p = (β_p - α_p) / (2 (-h_p(1))^{2/3}) of eq. (4.23): (θmax - location) / scale converges in distribution to TracyWidom(2). The standardization is validated against simulation at p = 50–200: standardized mean and sd match the Tracy-Widom(2) values -1.7711 and 0.9018 within Monte Carlo error at p ≥ 100.

Use the finite-p coefficient, not the closed form of Corollary 3.2: the corollary's constant C (eq. (3.5)) gives the limiting scale 1 / (C p^{ν + 2/3}), which agrees with σ_p only to 1 + o(1), and the o(1) vanishes slowly (relative error $\sim 7 p^{-\nu}$ at b = 1). At ν = 1/2 the limiting scale is 2.36, 1.89 and 1.60 times σ_p at p = 50, 100, 200 — far too wide for quantiles or tests at any practical size — so it is deliberately not exposed; this function returns the finite-p scale.

Only the unbalanced regime ν > 0 is covered: ν = 0 is the balanced regime of Johnstone (2008), and the ensemble here is complex only. See jacobiTWCDF and jacobiTWThreshold for the corrected distribution function of Theorem 3.1.

Reference: Didier, Gkogkou, Lopez Santander & McLaughlin, "On the asymptotic distribution of the largest eigenvalue of unbalanced complex Jacobi ensembles", arXiv:2611.00577 (2026), eqs. (1.8), (2.12), (4.23).

source
RandomMatrix.jacobiTWCDF — Function
jacobiTWCDF(s, p, a, b, nu; corrected = true)

Theorem 3.1 of Didier et al. (2026): the corrected Tracy-Widom approximation to the distribution of the largest eigenvalue θmax of the unbalanced complex Jacobi ensemble of jacobiTWScaling,

\[P\bigl(\theta_{\max} \le \varphi_p(\chi_p^{-1}(s))\bigr) \ = \ F_2(s) \bigl(1 - 2\, \hat{C}_p\, p^{-2/3}\, v(s)\bigr) \ + \ O(p^{-1}),\]

where the threshold φ_p(χ_p⁻¹(s)) is jacobiTWThreshold, F_2 is the cdf of TracyWidom(2), Ĉ_p is the explicit coefficient of eq. (4.7), and v(s) = ( (∫_s^∞ q²)² - q(s)² ) / 2 for the Hastings-McLeod Painlevé II function q (eq. (3.2); the same q tabulated for TracyWidom). With corrected = false, returns the plain Tracy-Widom value F_2(s), whose error is the displayed p^{-2/3} term itself.

The correction term is genuinely small at practical sizes — at most about 0.003 of cdf at p = 50, ν = 1/2, shrinking like p^{-2/3} (simulation confirms its predicted size and decay) — so the theorem's O(p^{-1}) remainder, not the displayed term, is the dominant finite-p error; the standardization of jacobiTWScaling is the part that matters most in practice.

source
RandomMatrix.jacobiTWThreshold — Function
jacobiTWThreshold(s, p, a, b, nu)

The threshold φ_p(χ_p⁻¹(s)) of Theorem 3.1 of Didier et al. (2026): the value t such that P(θmax ≤ t) is approximated by jacobiTWCDF(s, p, a, b, nu), for the largest eigenvalue θmax of the unbalanced complex Jacobi ensemble of jacobiTWScaling. Here φ_p(λ) = (α_p + β_p)/2 + (β_p - α_p) λ/2 is the affine map onto the equilibrium support [α_p, β_p] (eq. (2.13)) and χ_p is the local Airy map of eq. (2.14), inverted here by bisection. jacobiTWThreshold(0, ...) is exactly the location β_p.

An approximate level-u quantile of θmax is jacobiTWThreshold(s_u, p, a, b, nu) with s_u solving jacobiTWCDF(s_u, p, a, b, nu) = u; to leading order s_u = quantile(TracyWidom(2), u).

source

At $p = 100$, $a = b = 1$, $\nu = 1/2$ the edge sits at $\beta_p \approx 0.399$ with scale $\sigma_p \approx 0.0071$:

using RandomMatrix, Distributions
ls = jacobiTWScaling(100, 1.0, 1.0, 0.5)
(location = round(ls.location, digits = 4), scale = round(ls.scale, digits = 5))
(location = 0.3994, scale = 0.00707)

Theorem 3.1 refines $P(\theta_{\max} \le \varphi_p(\chi_p^{-1}(s)))$ beyond the plain $F_2(s)$: at $s = -1$ the correction moves the cdf from 0.8072 to 0.8086 (the term is genuinely of order $p^{-2/3}$, and small), and the threshold map turns Tracy–Widom quantiles into approximate quantiles of $\theta_{\max}$ — the 95% point is $\approx 0.398$:

(plain = round(jacobiTWCDF(-1.0, 100, 1.0, 1.0, 0.5; corrected = false), digits = 4),
 corrected = round(jacobiTWCDF(-1.0, 100, 1.0, 1.0, 0.5), digits = 4),
 q95 = round(jacobiTWThreshold(quantile(TracyWidom(2), 0.95), 100, 1.0, 1.0, 0.5), digits = 4))
(plain = 0.8072, corrected = 0.8086, q95 = 0.3977)

One warning, also spelled out in the docstring of jacobiTWScaling: the paper's Corollary 3.2 gives a closed-form limiting scale as well, but it agrees with the finite-$p$ scale only to $1 + o(1)$ with a slowly vanishing error — at $\nu = 1/2$ it runs 1.6–2.4 times too wide at $p = 50$–$200$. The package exposes only the finite-$p$ scale.

Sampling the β-ensembles

BetaHermite and BetaLaguerre sample the full spectrum of a Gaussian / Wishart β-ensemble at any $\beta > 0$, from the Dumitriu–Edelman tridiagonal models — the same construction behind the general-β rand of TracyWidom. On the $1/\sqrt{n}$ scale the Hermite bulk follows the semicircle law on $[-2, 2]$ (extremes near $\pm 2$, scaled second moment near $1$):

λ = rand(BetaHermite(2, 400))
(round(minimum(λ) / sqrt(400), digits = 2),
 round(maximum(λ) / sqrt(400), digits = 2),
 round(mean(λ .^ 2) / 400, digits = 2))
(-1.96, 1.96, 1.0)

For the Laguerre ensemble the eigenvalues on the $1/n$ scale follow MarchenkoPastur(p/n) — here $p/n = 1/4$, so the mean is $1$ and the upper edge is $(1 + 1/2)^2 = 2.25$:

μ = rand(BetaLaguerre(1, 400, 100)) ./ 400
(round(mean(μ), digits = 3), round(maximum(μ), digits = 2))
(1.002, 2.19)
